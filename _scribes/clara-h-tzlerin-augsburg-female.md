    short_biography: Professional, laywoman scribe Hatzlerin was a scribe in Augsburg
      who has nine signed surviving manuscripts. She lived with her brother, a notary,
      and was commissioned to produce books for wealthy citizens. The article concludes
      with a descriptive catalog of her nine known manuscripts. Clara Hätzlerin (*
      around 1430 in Augsburg ; † probably 1476 or the following year) was a hired
      clerk in the 15th century. She is the only woman documented who made handwritten
      copies of German codices for a fee . It can be proven in Augsburg tax books
      between 1452 and 1476. [1] Clara Hätzlerin came from a family of notaries that
      was typical of Augsburg at the time. Her father Bartholomew is known to have
      been a so-called letter writer who, as a representative of rich citizens, regulated
      their legal rights. He is documented in tax books between 1409 and 1443 as well
      as through an entry in the Augsburg Missive Book and as a witness in a mortgage
      note from Marx Lang. [2] When Bartholomew died in 1444/45, Bartholomew's eldest
      son, who is documented as a notarius publicus between 1451 and 1496, took over
      the office and thus also the tax liability. The most conclusive refutation is
      provided by Augsburg tax books, which show that Clara paid taxes in her father's
      house for 24 years, something she could never have done as a nun, since in this
      case her assets would have gone to the monastery. [4] The profession of paid
      and professional writer is a phenomenon of the 15th century, a time in which
      the writing industry flourished and the need for everyday literature increased.
      The city of Augsburg played a pioneering role in this area, as it has a list
      of over 30 proven payroll clerks. Hired scribes were mainly laypeople who produced
      cheap copies of manuscripts for the public by copying them by hand and were
      no longer, as was usual up until then, members of the clergy who had specialized
      in the artistic design of magnificent manuscripts. In addition to Clara Hätzlerin,
      the calligraphers Heinrich Molitor and Heinrich Lengefeld and Konrad Bollstatter
      also belonged to this group of people. [5] It was quite common for professional
      writers to work part-time in public, legal or administrative writing. Clara
      Hätzlerin also worked in the law firm of her father Bartholomäus Hätzler and
      her brother of the same name. As a part-time job , she made handwritten copies
      of commissioned books. Some manuscripts from her pen, which are mainly practical
      manuscripts, have been preserved to this day. Clara wrote her texts in a very
      readable and carefully crafted script , which is often cited by paleographers
      as the prototype of the 15th-century chancellery bastarda . Last but not least,
      the consistent naming of scribes at the end of all codices made it possible
      for them to be included in the DAmalS [6] project of the Institute for German
      Studies at the Karl Franzens University of Graz . Medievalists there try to
      identify the scribes' hands of a manuscript using databases and use the Hätzler's
      manuscripts as a paleographic comparison option. To date, eight of the Hätzler's
      manuscripts are known. In the research literature the number nine is occasionally
      given when the two-part manuscript of “The Holy Lives” is counted as two works.
      These include legal books , hunting literature, a work on the subject of mysticism
      and magic and the collective manuscript “Song Book”. The eight codices date
      from the period between 1465 and 1473, were written in a script typical of Clara
      Hätzlerin, namely the Chancellery Bastarda , and could undoubtedly be attributed
      to Clara Hätzlerin as the scribe, as she signed most of the manuscripts herself
      . [2]
